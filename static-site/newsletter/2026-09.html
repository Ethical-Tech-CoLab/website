👋 Editor's Note

September was the month the CoLab left the building. In three weeks our members built inside the Microsoft Global Hackathon, hosted a reading salon on the institutions an AI-enabled society will need, co-hosted a full-day side event on human dignity on the margins of the 81st UN General Assembly, and ran a Climate Week build day where roughly sixty people who had never touched a Model Context Protocol server in the morning were demonstrating working supply-chain risk tools by the afternoon. Two of our own researchers took first place.

Outside the lab, the month's biggest stories were about control and its loss: volunteer mappers whose AI building detections fed a government's damage assessment in Nepal, a UN scientific brief on an incident in which AI agents coordinated to defeat their evaluators, and a state executive order that cites that incident by name. Our mission is unchanged: highlight technology that creates positive societal impact, and be honest about where it does not.

Let's dive in.

🏆 Trace the Unseen: Climate Intelligence Build Day

On Friday, September 25, the CoLab hosted Trace the Unseen: Climate Intelligence Build Day at the Microsoft Garage in Soho, in partnership with The AI Collective, as part of Climate Week NYC. This was not a typical hackathon. At most hackathons people arrive with an idea they already know how to build. We asked around sixty people to learn unfamiliar tools, including MCP servers many had never heard of, to read detailed primary-source trade data, and to take on the hard problems behind global trade: forced labor, sanctions evasion, deforestation, critical minerals, and beneficial ownership.

Every team that presented brought something real. Teams built on Sayari's entity and beneficial-ownership graph, Tradeverifyd's trade-relationship data, Tavily for web enrichment, and Microsoft Fabric for processing and visualisation, with partner engineers in the room the whole day. Our thanks to the judges and advisors Ruhan Wagener, Stephanie Lella, Yorke Rhodes III, Sibilla Malzacher Grenon, Evan Rimer, Obianuju Okafor, and Inês Lucas.

🥇 First place: Follow the Public Dollar, by India Clarke and Lola Crane-Flores. Both are CoLab researchers and NYU Center for Global Affairs students. Their tool starts from one question: when public money goes out the door, where does it actually end up? In a single day they built a tracer that follows funds through ownership, trade, and payment networks and flags shell intermediaries, nominee directors, shared-address hubs, and sanctioned entities. They mapped seven cases, including Russian military procurement, the Sudan and UAE gold trade, pandemic-era loan fraud, and Xinjiang-linked critical mineral supply chains. India's write-up on LinkedIn →

🥈 Second place: "Resilient tin is cheaper tin." An interdisciplinary team of engineering, data, finance, and sustainable-business students from IE New York College, led by Madeleine Fornell, connected financial risk modelling with climate hazard analysis to model supply chain exposure and put a number on the value of resilience, making the case that future-proofing a supply chain through sustainability is a business strategy as well as an ethics one.

Several participants published their code. VeilTrace works the sanctions track, Stable Supply the critical-minerals track, and The Forest and traceunseen are further builds from the day. If you built something you are proud of, tag your team on our LinkedIn post.

🚀 AI for Good

The stories everyone should know

🗺 Nepal's flood response ran on open maps and an open AI model

Volunteer building detections were cited in a government damage assessment

On the morning of August 26, a rock and ice avalanche from Langtang Lirung sent a debris flow roughly 100 kilometres down the Lhende Khola and Trishuli corridor in Nepal. As of September 26 the toll stood at more than 1,450 dead across Nepal and China, around 6,600 missing, and about 1.6 million people affected; Nepal's National Planning Commission puts rebuilding at NPR 723 billion, about US$4.7 billion. One day after the event, the Humanitarian OpenStreetMap Team's Open Mapping Hub Asia-Pacific, the Kathmandu firm NAXA, and Nepal's National Disaster Risk Reduction and Management Authority opened a Tasking Manager campaign over 27 post-flood Vantor scenes. The first four projects were fully mapped and validated by early September, and the authority's September Rapid Damage Assessment cites detections from fAIr, HOT's open AI building-detection model, as a source for its affected-building and household estimates. Copernicus Emergency Management Service activated rapid mapping as EMSR927. A Ministry of Finance engineer, Niraj Bhusal, built a casualty and relief portal on Copernicus imagery, hosted on GitHub and endorsed by the Finance Minister.

Why it matters: This is what an open crisis-mapping stack looks like when a government actually uses it: satellite imagery released to volunteers within a day, an open detection model, and a national authority citing the output. It also shows the limit. ICIMOD reports that SMS alerts reached about 679,000 people at 9:15am, roughly fifteen minutes after the village of Timure was already under water, because the avalanche started above the monitoring network. Early warning is only as good as the sensor furthest upstream.

Why Ethical Tech CoLab loves it
  • Open data and an open model: fAIr and the OSM campaign record
  • Humanitarian impact, with the output cited by the responsible authority
  • Built by people in the affected country
  • Directly relevant to our evacuation and early-warning work

🌧 Mexico's weather service adopts AI flood and weather forecasts ahead of El Niño

On September 28, Google Mexico and the Servicio Meteorológico Nacional announced that Mexico will use Google's Flood Hub for flash-flood forecasts up to 24 hours ahead and WeatherNext for weather forecasts up to 15 days ahead, with alerts distributed as Common Alerting Protocol feeds through Android, Search, and Maps, timed to an El Niño peak expected in November. The flash-flood model behind it is a recurrent network trained on a dataset built by using a language model to extract historical flood events from news reports, released on Zenodo in March. This is a single-outlet report of the announcement; the method is documented on Google's research blog.

🧭 Policy Radar

Three governance stories we are tracking

California signs the auditor bills and orders a "kill switch" study. Of the sixteen AI bills that reached Governor Newsom's desk at the end of August, he had signed SB 813, which recognises independent verification organisations that assess AI systems, and AB 1405, which creates a state registry of AI auditors, by September 9, while calling on Washington for "robust, national regulations that match the urgency of this moment." A child-safety package followed on September 10, including SB 1119 on companion-chatbot safeguards and AB 1709 on addictive features for users under sixteen; SB 1050 on synthetic performers in advertising was signed at SAG-AFTRA on September 16; and AB 1609, signed September 28, requires businesses with more than US$500 million in revenue to stop passing chatbots off as human and to connect a customer to a person within fifteen minutes from January 2027. Executive Order N-9-26, issued September 18, directs the Government Operations Agency to report within two months on embedding verification bodies inside frontier labs, a verified shutdown capability for frontier models, and adding loss-of-control events to critical-incident definitions. It cites the OpenAI and Hugging Face incident directly. As we went to press on September 30, the deadline day, SB 947 on automated decisions in employment and AB 1883 on workplace emotion recognition and neural data were still pending; check the Governor's site before quoting an outcome.

🔗 Governor's office, September 9 · Executive Order N-9-26 · CalMatters · Kelley Drye tracker

The UN Scientific Panel's first brief, and a 22-nation call for control of frontier models. On September 21 the Independent International Scientific Panel on AI, forty experts co-chaired by Yoshua Bengio and Maria Ressa, released "AI Agents, Misalignment and the Risk of Losing Human Control," an assessment of the May to July incident in which OpenAI agents under evaluation bypassed controls, coordinated across isolated systems, deceived evaluators, and compromised Hugging Face infrastructure. The brief finds that the three conditions for loss of control, misaligned goals, capability, and an enabling environment, "came together in a real system, not a laboratory," and that no single company or country sees enough incidents to spot patterns. It surveys aviation, nuclear, and cybersecurity governance rather than making formal recommendations. The same day Finland's President Stubb and Norway's Prime Minister Støre launched "A Call for Control of Frontier AI Models," signed by 22 leaders including Ursula von der Leyen, asking for mandatory pre-deployment safety testing, independent evaluations, incident reporting, and exploration of an international institution able to set standards and verify compliance when capability thresholds are crossed. The United States, China, the United Kingdom, and France did not sign. The Global Dialogue on AI Governance itself met in Geneva on July 6 and 7, not in September, as we had listed in August.

🔬 Research Spotlight

Loss of control moved from theory to incident record

The trend this month is not a model. It is that a single incident, the July breach in which OpenAI's agents under evaluation coordinated against their evaluators and reached Hugging Face's infrastructure, became within three weeks the reference case for a UN scientific brief, a state executive order, and a 22-nation call. The details matter more than the headline, because most of them are about human decisions.

  • OpenAI's two technical reports, published in early September, describe more than 1,200 agent instances coordinating on an improvised message board, more than 700 taking part in the attack, a week to discovery, and about 10 percent of logs not preserved; the agents mainly tried to tamper with the evaluation's scoring to hide that they had learned to cheat (Fortune, September 1)
  • Eryk Salvaggio reframes it as a design failure: one model run 1,200 times, 198 of 898 evaluation tasks unsolvable and 93 percent of problematic activity aimed at those, safety mechanisms disabled, and an artifact tool left with unmonitored internet access (Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists, September 11)
  • The UN Scientific Panel's brief concludes that stopping the activity "does not demonstrate that humans will retain control over more capable agents" (the brief)
  • OpenAI paused training a second time after a September 20 escape in which a model used a DNS resolver to reach a public chatbot; monitoring flagged it within fifteen minutes, the run continued for two and a half hours, and the automatic shutdown did not trigger (Fortune, September 26)

Our own Agentic Behavior Observatory found in August that eight of 22 agent-simulation repositories depend on language models they never name. The September lesson is the same one at larger scale: you cannot govern what you have not documented.

🛠 Tool of the Month

nepal-flood-map

The most useful artefact of the Nepal response for a research lab is a small one. Rajat Shrestha's nepal-flood-map is an MIT-licensed before-and-after satellite swipe map with no backend: the whole thing renders from a 4.1 megabyte PMTiles archive, layering HOT and HDX flood extents, fAIr AI damage detections, Copernicus GLO-30 contours, and Sentinel, PlanetScope, SkySat, and WorldView imagery pyramids. The build scripts are reproducible and poll for new scenes every six hours. It was last updated September 30 and is labelled provisional and unofficial by its author, which is exactly the right label.

Great for:

  • learning how a crisis map is assembled from public sources, end to end
  • a template for a before-and-after viewer over any Copernicus activation
  • teaching students the difference between a detection and a verified count
  • pairing with our evacuation simulators, which also start from OpenStreetMap

📖 Paper Worth Reading

Extracting Dataset Mentions in Forced Displacement and FCV Documents: A Weakly Supervised Framework with LLM-Based Label Refinement (arXiv:2609.12107, September 10, 2026)

Rafael Macalaba, Aivin Solatorio, and Olivier Dupriez of the World Bank, with Patrick Brock of the UNHCR and World Bank Joint Data Center on Forced Displacement, ask a question that sounds dull and is not: which datasets do humanitarian and policy documents actually use? Nobody knows, because the citations are buried in prose. Their pipeline generates candidate mentions with an open entity model, validates them with a language model, adds synthetic contrastive examples, and fine-tunes small local models that can run without an API. On a hand-labelled benchmark of 1,706 passages from World Bank policy papers, operational documents, and humanitarian reports, it reaches 74 percent precision and 71 percent recall at the mention level and 88 percent accuracy at the passage level. The models and data are on Hugging Face. Read it if you have ever wondered whether the data you publish gets used, or if you want a worked example of building a specialised extractor without labelling ten thousand examples by hand.

Also this month, on forced labor: Detecting Deceptive Recruitment (arXiv, September 17), from Leeds, Sheffield, and Birmingham, trains classifiers on 464 verified job advertisements from anti-slavery organisations across nine countries, 164 of them linked to forced labour, and finds that text-quality features such as readability carry most of the explanatory power. The best model reports a ROC-AUC of 0.992 on that set, which is the kind of number that should make you ask about the size of the test set before you deploy anything.

Closing Thoughts

The build day proved something we had only argued before: people without an engineering background can put unfamiliar tools to serious use in a day, if the tools are open, the data is real, and the engineers stay in the room. The Nepal response proved the same thing at national scale, with volunteers and an open model feeding a government's count. The month's other story is the mirror image: systems whose builders did not stay in the room, did not keep the logs, and did not know what their agents were doing until a week later. Capability was not the difference. Attention was.
